When LLMs Sit Above Diagnostic Tools: Unrealized Complementarity in Industrial Fault Diagnosis
Donghwan Kim
Independent Researcher
donhkim9714@korea.ac.kr
Abstract
Large language models are increasingly used as integration layers above specialized tools, but a stronger component does not necessarily produce a stronger combined system. Across five diagnostic datasets spanning bearing vibration, process monitoring, and semiconductor equipment, we study whether an LLM can reliably use external diagnostic information. Paired repeat calls separate advice effects from ordinary output instability. In all five, conflicting external diagnostic information overturned initially correct LLM judgments. Four datasets also provide direct integration comparisons. Across those four, none showed a consistent advantage for implicit LLM integration over the stronger standalone source. On a Tennessee Eastman confirmation set whose protocol was fixed before evaluation, unaided accuracy was 64.67%, implicit LLM–specialist integration reached 77.43%, and the specialist alone reached 83.33%. Specialist information improved the LLM by 12.8 percentage points (95% interval 9.7 to 15.9), yet the integrated output remained 5.9 points below the specialist (95% interval −12.0 to −0.7). A two-source selector oracle reached 92.76%, indicating case-level complementarity between the standalone predictions that the integrated output did not fully realize. The integrated output missed 140 of 295 specialist corrections (47.5%) but lost 15 of 99 initially correct LLM judgments (15.2%). The deficit remained under prompt and specialist sensitivity analyses. Among CWRU cases diagnosed correctly under both evidence presentations, task-aligned physical evidence yielded lower susceptibility estimates in six of seven models, with five model-specific intervals excluding zero. Increasing reasoning effort produced no reliable reduction in susceptibility to incorrect advice in five models. In a separate four-model TEP sensitivity analysis, higher reasoning did not provide clear evidence of resolving the integration problem. Source quality and integration quality should therefore be evaluated separately: an LLM integration layer should be compared with its stronger standalone component, not only with the unaided LLM.
1. Introduction
Large language models need not replace specialized diagnostic models to influence industrial decisions. A vibration classifier or a process monitor can remain the component that turns sensors into evidence. The LLM can instead combine outputs from these components. One context can hold a tool output, a sensor summary, an alarm, a maintenance record, a passage from a manual, and a technician’s note. The model may then prioritize cases, resolve competing hypotheses, or recommend an inspection. Process-systems research already proposes language models as supervisory or coordination layers above numerical tools, retrieved engineering knowledge, and maintenance evidence (
Gopaluni, Kotamraju, and Bhushan, 2026
;
O’Donncha et al., 2026
;
Liang and Sin, 2026
;
Yang et al., 2026b
). Once a model performs that role, it directly affects the final decision even though it is not the primary detector. We test this assumption in controlled diagnostic settings rather than in deployed plant systems.
Combining informative sources does not necessarily improve the final decision; effective integration requires case-level arbitration between them. Two sources are complementary when each is correct on some cases the other misses. Realizing that complementarity requires choosing appropriately between them. Learning-to-defer methods explicitly optimize such routing decisions. Source quality describes how well each source performs alone. Integration quality describes how well the procedure combines them into a final label. A strong specialist and a competent model can still produce a worse answer than the specialist alone if the integrator ignores a correction or copies an error. Our central question is whether an LLM integrator can turn complementary but imperfect sources into a decision that matches or exceeds the stronger source. The same question applies to other LLM agents that must choose which imperfect source to trust.
Prior work has documented advice following, tool over-use and under-use, and human–AI teams that underperform their stronger member. This study examines those behaviors when the language model itself combines two fallible predictive judgments: an evidence-based LLM assessment and an external specialist prediction.
The protocol separates instability from the effect of the external source. The model first labels the evidence alone. A second call, with no new information, shows how often that label changes by itself. A third call adds one external diagnosis, sometimes right and sometimes wrong. We ask whether the combined decision outperforms the better standalone source and whether incorrect advice changes an initially correct answer more often than ordinary test–retest variability does.
We study this problem across five diagnostic datasets spanning three industrial settings. In all five, conflicting external diagnostic information overturned some initially correct LLM judgments (Appendix Figure N1). CWRU, Paderborn, XJTU-SY, and Tennessee Eastman provide direct integration comparisons. Across these four datasets, none showed a consistent advantage for implicit LLM integration over the stronger standalone source. Lam 9600 instead provides a controlled-advice real-equipment check. The Tennessee Eastman study provides the primary held-out evaluation because its protocol and confirmation set were fixed before evaluation.
In the held-out TEP evaluation, implicit integration scored 77.43%. The unaided models scored 64.67%, so the specialist source helped by 12.8 points (95% interval 9.7 to 15.9). The specialist alone scored 83.33%. The integrated output was therefore 5.9 percentage points below the specialist (95% interval −12.0 to −0.7). Advice improved the language model, but the integrated output still underperformed the stronger source. A two-source selector oracle reached 92.76%.
Of 1,050 integrated predictions, 237 were incorrect. Most were missed corrections: in 140 rows the specialist was correct and the final prediction was not. Harmful acceptance of incorrect advice accounted for 15 rows. A strategy focused solely on reducing specialist reliance would therefore target the less frequent failure mode.
The deficit was also observed under a direct arbitration instruction, when the model was also given its stored unaided diagnosis, and with an alternative random-forest specialist that had been fixed as a development-stage candidate, so it was not specific to the original prompt or the selected logistic-regression specialist.
The effects of the evaluated interventions were task-dependent. Among CWRU cases correctly diagnosed under both evidence presentations, task-aligned physical evidence reduced susceptibility to incorrect advice for six of the seven evaluated models; across the fixed seven-model panel, the post hoc unweighted mean reduction was 24.6 percentage points (95% interval 10.5 to 40.5). Increasing the provider reasoning-effort setting produced no reliable reduction in susceptibility to incorrect advice in any of five evaluated models. An exploratory Lam 9600 analysis provides a real-equipment example of advice vulnerability.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we separate source quality from integration quality and evaluate an LLM integrator against the stronger standalone source. Paired repeat calls control for ordinary output instability. Second, we provide direct integration comparisons across three bearing datasets and the held-out TEP evaluation. Secondary sensitivity analyses, conducted in stages after that evaluation, include an arbitration condition, a prior-label condition that also included the stored unaided diagnosis, and an alternative-specialist condition. A separate real-equipment vulnerability check uses Lam 9600. Third, we test two interventions intended to reduce susceptibility to incorrect advice. Task-aligned physical evidence yielded lower harmful-switching estimates for six of the seven evaluated models. Higher reasoning did not reliably reduce CWRU advice susceptibility, and a four-model TEP sensitivity analysis did not provide clear evidence that it resolved the integration problem.
These results motivate a simple evaluation rule: compare an LLM integration layer directly with its stronger standalone component, not only with the unaided LLM.
2. Related work
Prior work shows that models can over-rely on or under-use tools, and that a combined system can underperform its better member. We study whether an LLM can benefit when each source is correct on some cases the other misses.
Dialogue studies show that assistants adopt a user’s stated preference and sometimes admit mistakes they did not make (
Perez et al., 2022
;
Sharma et al., 2023
).
Xie et al. (2024)
find that models can be receptive to coherent external evidence that conflicts with stored knowledge, and also that they continue to favor evidence consistent with that knowledge when both are present. In our setting, a specialist class label is provided alongside machine evidence, and success is accuracy against the better source, not agreement with a user.
Recent work shows that source identity and perceived authority can shape how LLMs resolve conflicting information.
Kumar and Chopra (2026)
find that attribution to a verified source induces more compliance than a matched user endorsement and that compliance increases with stated source authority.
Schuster, Gautam, and Markert (2026)
report preferences among source types in inter-context conflicts, while
Li et al. (2025)
find that models can favor user-provided claims over retrieved database evidence when the two conflict. These results indicate that source reliance depends on the setting and source type rather than following a universal authority ordering. These studies characterize source preference or compliance under conflict; we instead ask whether an LLM integrator can benefit when each source is correct on some cases the other misses, and match or exceed the stronger standalone source. For this setting, measuring only adoption of an incorrect source is insufficient because integration can also fail by ignoring a correct specialist when the LLM is wrong.
Tool evaluations already separate adopting a bad return from failing to use a needed one.
Yang et al. (2026a)
corrupt search, delegated answers, and code. Mean adoption of the corrupted return is high for every tool; models sometimes notice the conflict and still return the corrupted answer.
Soni (2026)
scores a skipped tool, an ignored result, a fabricated addition, and an unnecessary call as different failures.
Xu et al. (2026)
define “Tool Ignored” as a tool-integrated math failure in which a correct code-execution result is overridden by the model’s own incorrect reasoning. Their calibration method uses confidence in the generated code to decide whether to trust the executor. In our setting, the external source is a fitted diagnostic model with its own error rate, so the integrator must avoid both ignoring correct labels and adopting incorrect ones. We measure under-use where the language model is the integrator and ask whether it can benefit when each source is correct on some cases the other misses.
Recent work has also framed tool use and retrieval as arbitration between fallible sources. MemToC evaluates conflicts between model-specific closed-book answers and executable-tool returns under controlled source correctness (
Varlamov et al., 2026
), while TrustMargin selects between parametric-memory and retrieval-based answers using a training-free arbitration score (
Xu, Shi, et al., 2026
). Our setting differs in both source type and evaluation target: the LLM diagnoses from industrial sensor or process evidence while the second source is a fitted diagnostic specialist whose errors arise naturally. We additionally control for ordinary output instability with paired repeat calls and evaluate whether prompt-level integration exceeds the stronger standalone component.
Human studies ask a person to accept or reject advice.
Schemmer et al. (2023)
separate relative reliance on correct AI advice from relative self-reliance when the advice is wrong.
Bansal et al. (2021)
ask whether explanations help a human–AI team beat both the human and the model alone, and find that explanations can increase acceptance regardless of correctness.
Vaccaro, Almaatouq, and Malone (2024)
find that combinations perform worse than the better partner on average, especially on decisions. In contrast, we study settings in which the language model itself performs the integration.
Learning-to-defer and triage methods explicitly learn or optimize who handles each case (
Madras, Pitassi, and Zemel, 2018
;
Mozannar and Sontag, 2020
;
Okati, De, and Gomez-Rodriguez, 2021
;
Mozannar et al., 2023
). Our setting differs in the mechanism evaluated: we study an off-the-shelf LLM used directly as the integration layer, without a separately trained routing objective.
A similar distinction appears in ensemble studies.
Kim (2026a)
finds oracle gains across language-model subsets and realized majority-vote gains in only a small fraction of them.
Kim (2026b)
finds that a reference-free judge often fails to select a better table-recognition candidate than the first draft. The improvement possible case by case can be much larger than the improvement actually delivered by an aggregation rule. Unlike LLM ensemble settings, our second source is a non-LLM diagnostic model, and the LLM performs case-level integration rather than majority voting.
Industrial fault-diagnosis studies also show that how evidence is presented can matter.
Lee et al. (2025)
compare raw sensor windows with descriptive statistics and single- with multi-model diagnostic architectures.
Li and Zhao (2025)
convert industrial time series into textual trends, periodicity, and deviations before retrieval-guided zero-shot diagnosis. Those studies evaluate diagnostic accuracy and explanation. Our evidence-representation result instead asks whether a model that can already solve a case becomes less likely to abandon that answer after incorrect advice.
Other industrial systems place language models above tools or heterogeneous evidence. FaultExplainer explains principal-component alarms on the Tennessee Eastman process (
Khan et al., 2025
).
Liang and Sin (2026)
use a language model to coordinate convolutional and ensemble diagnostic tools on the same process and evaluate diagnosis, tool adherence, and report quality. KDACL combines process data, retrieved mechanistic knowledge, and iterative verification to construct causal chains on Tennessee Eastman and a slag-mill system (
Yang et al., 2026b
). AgentRCA pairs a tool-using model with a model of normal dynamics to compare fault hypotheses (
Wei and Fink, 2026
), while
O’Donncha et al. (2026)
combine maintenance records, operational summaries, and failure knowledge in a verification-constrained decision-support layer. These systems establish the practical relevance of the integration-layer role. They mainly evaluate pipeline diagnosis, reporting, causal-chain quality, or controlled deployment. We instead ask whether the final label retains the stronger source’s performance while benefiting from cases where the other source is correct.
3. Experimental framework
3.1 Experimental objective
The experiment evaluates whether an LLM can appropriately arbitrate between two imperfect sources at the case level. It does not ask the model to replace the specialist detector (Figure 1). Source quality is the accuracy of each source alone. Integration quality is the accuracy of the procedure that produces the final label. We define a two-source selector oracle that is correct whenever either stored standalone prediction is correct. It represents the maximum accuracy attainable by selecting between these two stored labels and is not an implemented integration method.
Figure 1:
The language model is an integration layer, not a replacement detector. Left: machine evidence, a specialist model, and records or human notes can enter one context, and the model may prioritize cases or recommend an action. Right: the paired repeat measures ordinary test–retest instability, while the advised call measures the effect of an external diagnosis beyond that baseline.
Each case has a true class and a block of evidence that does not contain the label. The model returns one class from a fixed list. Three calls use that evidence, with the same decoding settings. The first is unaided. The second is an independent repeat with no new information. The repeat is a separate API call with an identical prompt and identical decoding settings; it captures provider-side nondeterminism even at temperature 0. That paired repeat is necessary because a label can change on its own; without it, ordinary instability would be mistaken for the effect of the external source. The third call adds one sentence stating an external diagnostic label. We refer to this advised output as prompt-level implicit integration, or simply implicit integration below: the LLM receives both the case evidence and the external diagnostic prediction in context, without a separately trained routing or fusion mechanism. We then ask whether it outperforms the better of the two standalone sources and whether incorrect advice changes an initially correct judgment beyond ordinary repeat instability. Prompt templates and examples are given in Appendix C.

WM-811K is a separate interface-boundary pilot and is not counted among the five. The datasets serve distinct experimental roles rather than matched replications. Table 1 states the role of each. Bearing recordings are where the integration failure was characterized and where a change to the evidence representation was tested. The Tennessee Eastman confirmation protocol was fixed before evaluation and serves as the primary held-out non-bearing integration evaluation; Lam 9600 is an exploratory real-equipment check of advice vulnerability.

3.3 Models
The fixed panel is seven models, kept unchanged across paired calls for each model–condition combination: GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Qwen 3.8, GLM-5.3, and Kimi K3. Provider routing identifiers and settings are in Appendix A. The CWRU reasoning-effort comparison in Section 4.6 uses five of these models on the same cases and is not pooled with the other two. A separate TEP sensitivity analysis uses four of the seven models; the final reduced panel was determined before any confirmation outcome at the higher reasoning setting was examined.
3.4 Two kinds of external diagnosis
The external diagnosis is presented in fixed wording, but its source differs across experimental settings.
In the bearing susceptibility and intervention experiments, correctness is set by the design. The external diagnosis is written to be correct, or written to be incorrect, so that a change after incorrect advice can be compared with the paired repeat. Those are controlled advice conditions. Their purpose is to isolate the effect of a conflicting source. Incorrect labels follow the dataset-specific deterministic maps in Appendix Table C1.
Lam likewise uses controlled advice rather than predictions from a standalone diagnostic model. It therefore tests advice vulnerability, not source complementarity or integration-versus-stronger-source performance.
In the integration experiments, including the held-out TEP evaluation and the bearing integration comparisons, the external label is the actual prediction of a fixed specialist model. On the process confirmation set, the specialist is a multinomial logistic regression fit on training runs only. It was selected on development data from two predefined candidates using prespecified eligibility and ranking criteria. The criteria required enough errors and source disagreement to study case-level integration; without such cases, the sources offer little information about when either one can add value. Confirmation outcomes were not used. Appendix Table A1 reports the candidate values and full rule. In the fitted-specialist experiments, errors arise naturally from the specialist model rather than being experimentally assigned. Bearing integration comparisons use the six out-of-fold random forests specified in Section 3.2. This setting tests whether language-model integration benefits when each source is correct on some cases the other misses. Because the source wording and authority differ between controlled-advice and fitted-specialist experiments, we do not compare their absolute switching rates.
3.5 Study chronology
Bearing experiments motivated the questions of source integration and evidence representation. The Tennessee Eastman protocol, specialist-selection procedure, and confirmation set were fixed before the confirmation evaluation. The specialist was selected using development data only; confirmation outcomes played no role in selection. Development results are not added to confirmation results. After the primary analysis, the arbitration condition and the alternative-specialist condition were specified and fixed before their additional model calls. The prior-label condition was specified later, after those results were known, and was fixed before its own calls. It was therefore analyzed separately from the two earlier sensitivity conditions. The sensitivity analysis with higher reasoning effort was added after the primary and earlier secondary analyses. Its final reduced panel was determined before any confirmation outcome at the higher reasoning setting was examined.
3.6 Prompt and specialist sensitivity analyses
The three conditions reused the same 150 confirmation runs, seven LLMs, unaided outputs, and paired-repeat outputs. When each condition was specified is stated in Section 3.5.
The arbitration condition retained the selected logistic-regression specialist and changed only the advised prompt by adding one direct arbitration instruction:
You have two fallible sources: your own reading of the case
evidence above and the external diagnostic model’s prediction.
Decide which diagnosis is better supported by the case evidence.
Do not automatically defer to either source.
We treat this prompt as a sensitivity condition rather than an optimized or calibrated fusion procedure. The prior-label condition repeats that arbitration instruction. The specialist prediction remains the preceding case-specific line,
An external diagnostic advisor suggests: <specialist>.
The inserted line is placed after the first arbitration sentence and immediately before the decision instruction:
Your earlier diagnosis from the case evidence alone: <z0>.
That order was fixed for all cases and was not randomized. The alternative-specialist condition retained the primary implicit-integration interface but replaced the selected logistic specialist with the alternative 400-tree random forest. That random forest was one of the two candidates fixed during development, passed the same eligibility criteria, and was not selected for the primary test under the prespecified ranking rule. Full protocol details and results are in Appendix K. A separate sensitivity analysis with higher reasoning effort is reported in Section 4.6 and Appendix E.2.
3.7 Evidence representations
On CWRU every request uses an anonymous case identifier and one final evidence block. The generic text is a raw-spectrum summary: band energy, a dominant peak, time-domain shape statistics, and coarse energy shares around precomputed BPFO, BPFI, and BSF bands. It does not provide the bearing geometry, the characteristic frequencies themselves, envelope peaks or harmonics, or an explicit mapping from those frequencies to candidate faults. The task-aligned view adds information absent from the generic representation, including the envelope spectrum at the characteristic frequencies, the theoretical frequency values, and explicit mappings between frequencies and candidate faults. The two views therefore do not carry the same information. Advice is presented in fixed wording, and the model, decoding settings, class list, and response schema are held fixed across paired conditions. The common-correct analysis in Section 4.5 compares cases the model already diagnosed correctly under both views.

3.8 Inclusion criteria for bearing comparisons
A bearing comparison enters the count in Section 4.4 only if it meets the following predefined inclusion rules. Unaided accuracy must exceed chance by at least 0.15: 0.40 on the four-class CWRU task and about 0.48 on the three-class bearing tasks. We also exclude comparisons when the accuracy gap between the selector oracle and the better source is under 0.03 or the specialist is incorrect on fewer than 15 cases; there is then too little evidence to estimate the integration-versus-source difference reliably. Twenty-two comparisons meet these rules. The CWRU specialists do not enter that count because each made fewer than 15 errors. All 28 CWRU point estimates were still below the stronger source. The rules came from the bearing verification protocol and were not chosen from the comparisons summarized here. The 22-comparison count is conditional on them.

4. Results
4.1 Implicit LLM integration remains below the specialist
Implicit LLM integration scored 5.9 percentage points below the specialist alone, even though access to the specialist prediction improved the unaided LLM by 12.8 points (95% interval 9.7 to 15.9).
Seven models saw the same generic numerical summary of 150 Tennessee Eastman runs. Unaided accuracy was 64.67%, implicit integration reached 77.43%, and the specialist alone reached 83.33% (Figure 2). The gain of the integrated output over the unaided models was 12.8 points, with a supplementary run-bootstrap interval from 9.7 to 15.9. The primary contrast, integrated output minus specialist, is −5.9 points, with a 95% interval from −12.0 to −0.7. The supplementary gain interval is entirely positive, while the primary interval for integrated accuracy minus specialist accuracy is entirely negative. Differences are calculated from the unrounded seven-model means; because every model has the same number of cases, those means match the pooled rates. 4.2 Most integration errors are missed corrections
The dominant observed failure was not broad adoption of incorrect advice. It was failure to use correct specialist information when the model was wrong.
Figure 3 separates the rows on which the two standalone sources were correct. The sources disagreed in 394 of 1,050 model–case rows: the specialist alone was correct in 295, and the unaided LLM alone was correct in 99. The integrated output missed 140 of the 295 specialist corrections (47.5%) and lost 15 of the 99 initially correct LLM judgments (15.2%). Fourteen of those 15 harmful acceptances copied the specialist’s wrong label, all in the normal class. Even when both standalone labels were correct, the separate advised call was wrong in nine rows. These are not source-disagreement errors; they show that agreement between the two input labels did not guarantee the same output on a new call. The remaining 73 integration errors occurred when both sources were wrong; in 67 of those rows, both sources had already produced the same wrong label.

4.4 Bearing studies rarely show reliable gains over the stronger source
Across the included bearing analyses, implicit integration rarely outperformed the stronger standalone source. Of 22 eligible comparisons, three intervals were below zero, eighteen included zero, and one was above zero (Section 3.8; Appendix F). All 28 CWRU point estimates were still below the stronger source. The intervals are not adjusted for multiple comparisons, and the pattern does not imply that every integrated output fell below the stronger source.
4.5 Task-aligned evidence reduces susceptibility to incorrect advice
We next evaluate task-aligned physical evidence as an intervention for reducing susceptibility to conflicting advice.
Task-aligned evidence reduced susceptibility to incorrect advice across most models in the CWRU common-correct analysis. We measure the extent to which initially correct diagnoses are switched after incorrect advice, adjusting for ordinary paired test–retest instability. The comparison is restricted to cases correctly diagnosed under both evidence presentations.
Six of seven point estimates were positive, and five confidence intervals excluded zero (Figure 5). Estimates ranged from 0.00 to 0.48; model-specific values, intervals, and common-correct sample sizes are shown in Figure 5 and Appendix D. As a post hoc summary across the fixed seven-model panel, the unweighted mean reduction was 0.246 [0.105, 0.405] under a joint file-cluster bootstrap.

Paderborn and XJTU provide supporting bearing-domain evidence, with changes in diagnostic competence accompanying the changes in susceptibility to incorrect advice (Appendix H). The same benefit did not appear when the TEP development measurements were grouped into deterministic subsystem summaries (difference −1.7 percentage points, 95% CI −3.6 to +0.3; Appendix I). The CWRU result therefore supports task-aligned evidence presentation as an intervention that helps in this setting, not a general benefit of adding or reorganizing structure.
4.6 Higher reasoning effort does not clearly resolve the integration deficit
We next tested whether a higher reasoning effort setting reduced susceptibility to incorrect advice. On CWRU, it did not yield a statistically reliable reduction for any of the five evaluated models. Neither unaided accuracy nor susceptibility to incorrect advice showed a reliable improvement across those five models: every model-specific interval for the low-to-high change included zero (Appendix E).
Because the primary held-out TEP evaluation used the low reasoning-effort setting, we conducted a sensitivity analysis with higher reasoning effort on four of the seven models. The final four-model panel was determined before any confirmation outcome at the higher reasoning setting was examined; three models were excluded because development checks did not reliably distinguish the requested high setting from the low setting. Among the four retained models, higher reasoning increased unaided accuracy by 5.2 percentage points (95% interval 2.2 to 8.2), whereas integrated accuracy increased by 2.2 points (−0.2 to 4.8). The difference between these two changes was itself uncertain (Appendix E.2). At the high setting, the integrated output remained 4.2 points below the better standalone source (−10.5 to 0.8). The high-setting errors showed the same qualitative asymmetry. The integrated output missed 69 of 134 available specialist corrections (51.5%), while harmful acceptance occurred in 11 of 58 LLM-only cases (19.0%); the selector oracle remained near 93%. Thus, increasing reasoning effort did not provide clear evidence of resolving the TEP integration problem. This reduced-panel analysis is a secondary sensitivity analysis rather than an independent confirmation. Model-level results are in Appendix E.
4.7 Exploratory evaluation on real-equipment data
An exploratory analysis using public Lam 9600 data from a commercial semiconductor etch tool provides a real-equipment example of advice vulnerability. Of 23 initially correct model–case judgments, six were overturned by incorrect external advice across four wafers, while the paired repeats did not flip. This analysis is descriptive and is not intended as a benchmark of diagnostic capability (Appendix J). Controlled labels make this a vulnerability check, not a test of source complementarity.
5. Discussion
5.1 Source quality is not integration quality
The integration problem was not confined to one benchmark. Across the four datasets with direct integration comparisons, implicit integration did not show a consistent advantage over the stronger standalone source. On Paderborn and XJTU-SY, only one of 22 eligible comparison intervals was above zero; all 28 CWRU point estimates were below the stronger source, although specialist errors were too sparse for comparable inference. The held-out TEP evaluation provides the strongest evidence.
A specialist source can make a language model more accurate and still leave the combined system below that specialist. In the held-out TEP evaluation, the unaided models scored 64.67%, implicit integration scored 77.43%, and the specialist alone scored 83.33%. The 12.8-point gain over the unaided LLM shows that the specialist label carried useful information that the models sometimes used. The 5.9-point deficit relative to the specialist shows that the integration procedure did not preserve the stronger source’s standalone performance. The selector oracle at 92.76% indicates that each standalone source was correct on some cases the other missed. Because the development selection criteria required enough disagreement to study integration, that 9.4-point difference describes this selected setting, not what should be expected from an arbitrary specialist.
Before placing an LLM integration layer above a validated specialist, the layer should itself be validated against that source. As a deployment check, the integrated output should at minimum be compared with the strongest source selected on development data; in this experiment that source retained 83.33% accuracy versus 77.43% for implicit integration. This comparison does not select, case by case, whichever standalone source was correct.
The result was also observed under a direct arbitration instruction, when the model was also given its stored unaided diagnosis in the prior-label condition, and with the alternative random-forest specialist. Giving the model its stored unaided diagnosis removed the remaining harmful acceptances and increased missed corrections further: 215 of 295 specialist corrections were missed, and 0 of 99 harmful acceptances remained. These sensitivity analyses do not establish failure for every prompting or fusion procedure, or for every specialist family, but they show that the primary finding is not specific to the original prompt or to the selected logistic-regression specialist.
5.2 Missed corrections dominate harmful acceptance
Strategies aimed only at limiting adoption of incorrect tool output address harmful acceptance but do not address the missed corrections that dominated the TEP error analysis. In the TEP evaluation, missed corrections substantially outnumbered harmful acceptances (140 vs. 15), indicating that under-use of correct specialist advice was the dominant failure mode. Incorrect advice still had an effect, but it was concentrated in a small number of models and classes. Evaluations that measure only adoption of incorrect specialist outputs would therefore overlook the dominant failure mode.
The explicit arbitration instruction illustrates the same tension: harmful acceptance decreased, but missed corrections increased. A rule that only suppresses tool following therefore need not improve case-level source selection.
The missed-correction pattern is consistent with the “Tool Ignored” failure reported for tool-integrated math reasoning (
Xu et al., 2026
), here observed when the external source is itself imperfect. The bearing comparisons did not show a single dominant direction of source-selection failure. In the process experiment, the integrated output often failed to use a correct specialist label, and those rows were common enough to dominate the error count. Across settings, case-level source selection was unreliable. The experiments do not show why models over-use advice in some settings and under-use it in others.
5.3 Interventions affecting susceptibility to incorrect advice
In our experiments, presenting task-aligned physical evidence reduced susceptibility to incorrect advice for most CWRU models. Higher reasoning effort did not yield a reliable reduction in that CWRU susceptibility. The TEP sensitivity analysis shows that an improvement in standalone accuracy need not translate into a clear improvement in source integration. Higher reasoning effort therefore should not be assumed to solve case-level source arbitration automatically. Reorganizing the same Tennessee Eastman measurements did not produce the same benefit (Section 4.5).
These results suggest three practical deployment checks: compare the integrated output with the stronger source, not only with the unaided model; measure missed corrections and harmful acceptances separately; and, as in the CWRU setting, test whether the evidence representation exposes the physical structure needed for diagnosis. Verification-constrained industrial systems show the value of constructing and checking evidence before producing recommendations (
O’Donncha et al., 2026
;
Yang et al., 2026b
). Our results add that the final system should also be compared directly with its stronger standalone component.
The exploratory Lam 9600 analysis extends the broader advice vulnerability to public data from a commercial semiconductor etch tool, but its controlled-advice design does not provide an integration-versus-source comparison.
6. Limitations
The study uses controlled interfaces for external diagnostic information. In the bearing advice experiments, the external diagnosis was presented in fixed wording and its correctness was set by design, so that the effect of a conflicting source could be separated from test–retest instability. In the integration experiments, including the held-out TEP evaluation, the external label came from a fixed specialist model with naturally occurring errors, but it was still presented as a short fixed diagnostic statement rather than a live engineer note or a full tool log.
The primary TEP condition evaluates implicit integration. The arbitration condition and the prior-label condition add a direct source-arbitration instruction; the prior-label condition also included the stored unaided diagnosis. Broader structured or learned fusion procedures were not evaluated. In the prior-label condition, the specialist prediction appeared first in the prompt and the model’s earlier diagnosis followed within the arbitration text, immediately before the decision instruction. This order was fixed for all cases and was not randomized. Because the earlier diagnosis immediately preceded the decision instruction, the design cannot distinguish anchoring on the earlier diagnosis from a positional or recency effect. One possible explanation is increased anchoring on the model’s prior judgment, but the experiment was not designed to isolate that mechanism. The primary TEP integration experiments used the low reasoning-effort setting. The sensitivity analysis with higher reasoning effort used four of the seven primary models because the requested high setting could not be reliably distinguished from low reasoning for three models during development checks. Even among the retained models, the operational strength of the manipulation varied by provider; Claude Opus 5.5, for example, reported zero reasoning tokens in 66% of high-setting confirmation calls. In that analysis, unaided accuracy improved, but the results did not establish whether the integration gap narrowed: the interval for integrated accuracy minus the better standalone source still included zero. The operational meaning of high was provider-specific, and the high setting also used a larger output-token limit than the primary low setting. The higher-reasoning analysis therefore tests provider-side reasoning effort under a concise-output interface; it does not test explicitly elicited written deliberation.
The primary test used the multinomial logistic specialist selected on development data, while a secondary analysis with the alternative random-forest specialist also showed the integration deficit. Both are classical diagnostic models, so other specialist families may produce different error profiles and integration behavior.
We evaluated source integration across four diagnostic datasets spanning three bearing benchmarks and the Tennessee Eastman process, with an additional real-equipment check of advice vulnerability on Lam 9600. The Lam check uses controlled advice rather than predictions from a standalone fitted specialist and therefore does not test complementarity. Tennessee Eastman provides the primary held-out non-bearing evaluation, but it is a simulated process rather than historical process data from an operating plant. Specialist mistakes and the copying of those mistakes were concentrated in a few classes and models, so the effect varied across classes and models.
The semiconductor observations use few wafers and three models. The wafer-map image comparison is one model and 50 cases. Both analyses are exploratory and should not be interpreted as benchmark evaluations. Some rendered XJTU prompts and WM-811K image payloads were not retained, which limits exact prompt-level reproduction of those auxiliary analyses.
The design does not show whether baseline diagnostic accuracy causes models to over- or under-rely on advice. The bearing and process results both show poor case-level source selection, but they do not explain why the direction differs.
7. Broader impacts
Industrial diagnostic decisions can affect equipment availability, maintenance, and safety. The LLM layers evaluated here should not be deployed autonomously; our results argue for validating any such layer against the strongest available source.
8. Conclusion
Source quality and integration quality are distinct. Across four diagnostic datasets with direct integration comparisons, prompt-level implicit LLM integration did not consistently outperform the stronger standalone source. In the held-out TEP evaluation, specialist information substantially improved the unaided LLM, yet the integrated output remained below the specialist, with missed corrections dominating harmful acceptance. Task-aligned physical evidence reduced susceptibility to incorrect advice in the CWRU setting, whereas higher reasoning effort did not clearly resolve the TEP integration deficit. These results motivate evaluating LLM integration layers directly against their stronger standalone components rather than only against unaided LLMs.

Appendix G. Auxiliary evidence-strength analysis
We additionally tested whether an independently measured evidence-strength margin predicted susceptibility to incorrect advice. The model never received this margin. The final CWRU estimate was directionally positive but imprecise (OR 1.478, 95% CI [0.789, 2.597]). It was estimated from 206 eligible model–case rows, 22 file clusters, and seven models.
The relationship was not observed in the held-out TEP evaluation (OR 0.923, 95% CI [0.746, 1.142]). That analysis conditions on initially correct unaided predictions and is estimated from 688 model–case rows from 125 runs. One fault class contributed no initially correct rows, and one model contributed no harmful switches on its eligible rows. The exchangeable generalized estimating equation did not return a finite fit; the prespecified independence working correlation was therefore used with the same mean model.
Appendix H. Paderborn and XJTU supporting analyses
Paderborn and XJTU use related, but not identical, task-aligned interfaces. On Paderborn, model accuracy under the generic presentation was too low for a clean comparison of susceptibility to incorrect advice. The task-aligned presentation raised diagnostic accuracy and reduced harmful switching, so the two changes cannot be separated cleanly. A supporting Sol common-correct contrast was 0.758 [0.538, 0.917] on 33 cases from 13 bearings.
On XJTU, task-aligned evidence lowered the all-case harmful-switching rate for all seven evaluated models, while balanced diagnostic accuracy also increased. For several models, few cases were correct under both presentations because only 11 physical bearings were available. These results are consistent with the bearing-domain evidence-presentation finding, while diagnostic competence and susceptibility change together and CWRU remains the cleaner fixed-case analysis.
Appendix I. TEP development presentation contrast
During development, the same semantic process measurements were shown either as the generic numerical sheet or reorganized deterministically by subsystem and pathway. Retest-adjusted harmful switching was 0.107 under the generic presentation and 0.124 under the subsystem presentation, a difference of −0.017 (95% CI [−0.036, 0.003]). This development result was not combined with the prespecified confirmation set.
Appendix J. Real-equipment and interface boundaries
In the Lam five-class task, 23 of 60 unaided model–case judgments (three models by 20 wafers) were correct. Six were changed to the incorrect external label, across four wafers, while their paired repeats did not flip. The result is descriptive.
In the 50-case WM-811K pilot, the best text-interface accuracy was 0.36. The same maps shown natively as images to one model reached 0.72, compared with about 0.70 for a numeric classifier. This pilot suggests an interface limitation and should not be interpreted as a general image-model result.
Appendix K. TEP secondary sensitivity analyses
The arbitration condition and the alternative-specialist condition were specified after the primary confirmation analysis and fixed before 2,100 additional advised-only calls. That specification reused the fixed 150-run confirmation set, seven LLMs, and the primary unaided and paired-repeat outputs. Ten thousand percentile-bootstrap samples resampled confirmation runs jointly across the fixed seven-model panel with seed
20260924
. The prior-label condition was specified later, after the arbitration and alternative-specialist results were known, and was fixed before its own calls. It was therefore analyzed separately from the two earlier sensitivity conditions.
The arbitration condition retained the logistic specialist and added exactly:
You have two fallible sources: your own reading of the case
evidence above and the external diagnostic model’s prediction.

Appendix N. Advice vulnerability across datasets
Figure N1 summarizes advice vulnerability across the five datasets. Each point conditions on an initially correct unaided judgment and an incorrect external diagnosis. Paired-repeat retention measures ordinary instability; advised retention measures the corresponding outcome after conflicting external information. The panels use different external-diagnosis regimes and should not be compared in absolute level. No cross-dataset pooling or inference is performed.

Advice vulnerability across five diagnostic datasets. Panel A shows fixed generic controlled wrong-advice conditions for CWRU, Paderborn, and XJTU-SY.
